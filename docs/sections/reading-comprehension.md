# Reading comprehension

**In one line:** you read a short technical passage or a written procedure and answer questions about what it says, what it means and what follows from it.

Reading shows up under different names. Steamfitters Local 601 asks for Accuplacer Reading. Locals using WorkKeys, such as Local 22 in Western New York, ask for Workplace Documents, which is reading in a work setting. Locals using an aptitude battery often include a reading part. On the job, reading is how you follow a spec, a safety procedure or an installation manual, so committees treat it as seriously as math.

## What it measures

- finding a stated detail quickly
- picking out the main idea or purpose of a passage
- following the order of steps in a procedure
- drawing a conclusion the passage supports, without adding outside knowledge
- working out the meaning of a word from how it is used

## How the questions look

A passage of one or two paragraphs, sometimes a list of steps, followed by several multiple-choice questions. Topics are often practical: equipment, safety, materials or a work procedure. You do not need to know the topic in advance. Everything you need is in the passage.

| Question type | What it asks | Clue words |
|---|---|---|
| Detail | What the passage says about one thing | "According to the passage" |
| Main idea | What the whole passage is about | "mainly", "best title", "purpose" |
| Sequence | What comes before or after a step | "first", "after", "next" |
| Inference | What the passage supports but does not state | "suggests", "best supported", "can be concluded" |
| Vocabulary | What a word means here | "As used in the passage" |

## A method that works

1. **Read the questions first, quickly.** Then you know what to look for while you read.
2. **Read the passage once, for the point.** After each paragraph, say in five words what it was about.
3. **Answer from the text, not from memory.** For every answer, put your finger on the sentence that proves it. If you cannot find one, it is probably wrong.
4. **For main idea questions, test each option against the whole passage.** The right answer covers everything. Wrong ones are usually too narrow (one detail) or too broad (more than the passage says).
5. **For sequence questions, number the steps** in the margin as you read.
6. **For inference questions, choose the answer that needs the smallest leap.** The test wants what the passage clearly supports, not what might be true.

## Traps that cost points

- **The true-but-not-stated answer.** An option can be correct in real life and still be wrong because the passage does not say it. Only the passage counts.
- **The right words, wrong meaning.** Wrong options often reuse words from the passage in a changed sentence. Check the meaning, not the vocabulary.
- **Mixing up two similar things.** If a passage compares two devices, read carefully which statement belongs to which.
- **Extreme words.** Options with "always", "never" or "only" are wrong more often than not, unless the passage uses the same word.
- **Skipping the procedure's exceptions.** "Repeat from step three" is not the same as "start again".

## Timing

Spend about two minutes reading a passage the first time and 30 to 45 seconds on each question. A careful first read pays for itself, because you will not need to reread for every question. If time is very short, read the questions, then scan for the keywords they mention.

## Practice items

These 4 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from any local's test, publisher or prep site. The passage is followed by four questions. Use only the passage to answer.

### Passage 1

> When a valve in a water line closes suddenly, the moving water has nowhere to go. Its momentum turns into a pressure spike that travels back along the pipe, often with a loud bang. This is called water hammer. Repeated spikes can loosen joints, damage valves and shorten the life of a water heater. Fast-closing valves, such as those on washing machines and dishwashers, are common causes.
>
> Installers reduce the problem in two main ways: by securing pipes so they cannot move, and by fitting a small arrestor near the fast-closing valve. An arrestor contains a sealed air cushion or piston that absorbs the spike. Older homes sometimes have air chambers, short vertical pipes capped at the top. They work on the same idea, but they slowly fill with water and stop working unless they are drained.

**Item 1.** What is the main purpose of the passage?

A. To explain why washing machines break down · B. To describe what water hammer is, what harm it does and how it is reduced · C. To argue that air chambers should be removed from older homes · D. To compare the cost of arrestors and air chambers

**Item 2.** According to the passage, which of these is a common cause of water hammer?

A. Pipes that are too large · B. A water heater set too hot · C. Air trapped in an arrestor · D. Fast-closing valves on some appliances

**Item 3.** Why do air chambers stop working, according to the passage?

A. Their caps come loose. · B. They slowly fill with water. · C. Their pistons wear out. · D. They are installed too far from the valve.

**Item 4.** Which statement is best supported by the passage?

A. Arrestors must be drained regularly. · B. Water hammer happens only in older homes. · C. A pressure spike can travel along the pipe away from the valve that caused it. · D. Dishwashers should not be connected to the water supply.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 4</summary>

**Item 1: B.** The first paragraph says what water hammer is and what damage it does; the second says how installers reduce it. B covers both. *Why not A:* washing machines are mentioned only as one cause. *Why not D:* cost is never mentioned.

**Item 2: D.** The passage says "fast-closing valves, such as those on washing machines and dishwashers, are common causes". None of the other options is mentioned as a cause.

**Item 3: B.** Air chambers "slowly fill with water and stop working unless they are drained". *Why not C:* pistons are part of some arrestors, not of air chambers.

**Item 4: C.** The spike "travels back along the pipe". *Why not A:* the passage says air chambers need draining, not arrestors, which are sealed. *Why not B:* older homes are mentioned only in connection with air chambers.

</details>
